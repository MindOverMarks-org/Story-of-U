The Evidence Room · Case 01

Milgram’s obedience studies

Evidence labels (Well supported, Mixed evidence, Open question, Interpretation) apply to one claim at a time. They are not a rating of the whole study. Small grey tags next to a source say how far it was checked; the References page explains.

Content note. This case is about people being pressured to hurt someone. No one was actually hurt. If that is upsetting, skip to “What we can conclude” or go to Find Support.
The question

In the early 1960s, Stanley Milgram asked how ordinary people behave when an authority tells them to harm another person.

Claims, one at a time
Well supported

What happened

Participants were told to give a “learner” (an actor) stronger and stronger electric shocks during a learning task. No real shocks were given. Milgram reported that 26 of the 40 participants gave the highest shock and 14 stopped earlier, and that some showed signs of strong tension.

Well supported

Where “65%” comes from

26 ÷ 40 = 65%. It describes one condition, not people in general. Milgram ran many variations, and the rate of full obedience varied dramatically between them.

Interpretation

Why people continued

Milgram’s own framing was obedience to authority. Reicher, Haslam and Smith argue that identification with the experimenter and the science explains more. Their evidence is a rating study: 32 academic psychologists and 96 first-year psychology students rated 15 variants, and the ratings predicted obedience rates. That is not a new experiment, and the question is still debated.

Mixed evidence

Would it happen today?

Burger ran a partial replication of one condition with 70 adults, stopping at 150 volts for safety. About 70% continued past that point, compared with about 82.5% in Milgram’s comparable condition. The difference was not statistically significant. Participants were screened, outcomes beyond 150 volts were estimated rather than observed, and commentators questioned how comparable it was.

Open question

What the archives show

Gina Perry, a writer and psychologist, argues from Milgram’s archived papers and interviews that his published accounts left out important details. Her conclusions are themselves debated. We have only read press coverage of the book, so treat this claim as unchecked.

Interpretation

Ethics

Baumrind argued in 1964 that participant welfare and debriefing were inadequate and that the method might not tell us about real life. Milgram published a reply the same year. Many researchers think the original procedure would not meet today’s ethics standards; no single source is cited for that view.

What we can conclude

Under strong pressure in this setting, many participants continued. The setting and how the situation was framed changed the outcomes.

What we cannot conclude

That people are naturally obedient, that one person’s behaviour shows their character, or that these results explain historical atrocities.

The Self, Unlabelled

Who are you without the label?

ME?
Explanation

Self-concept

Self-concept means how you describe and evaluate yourself. This page makes no research claim about it. It is a prompt to think about, not a finding.

Interpretation

Belonging

Baumeister and Leary reviewed existing evidence in 1995 and argued that people have a strong need for stable, caring relationships. It is a theoretical review, widely discussed, and not a final answer.

Mixed evidence

Stereotypes and performance

Steele and Aronson (1995) reported four studies in which Black participants did worse on a difficult verbal test when it was presented as measuring ability. A later meta-analysis of studies with girls on maths, science and spatial tests found a small average effect and signs of publication bias, so the idea is contested. The two sets of studies involve different groups and should not be merged.

Explanation

Averages are not individuals

A group average says little about any one person, because people within groups differ a lot. This is a basic statistical point, not a finding from one study.

Find Support · The Open Door

Starting the conversation

Pick one trusted adult: a parent, relative, teacher or school counsellor. You can say: “I’ve been struggling and I’d like help working out what to do.” You do not need to explain everything at once. For a qualified professional, look for a registered psychologist or psychiatrist in your area, and ask about their training.

What to expect

A first conversation is mostly listening and questions about what is going on and what would help. You choose what to share. Ask early how privacy works, because it differs by service, and workers may involve others if someone is at risk of serious harm. You can ask to speak to someone else if it does not feel right.

Supporting a friend

Listen, stay with them, and encourage them to tell a trusted adult. You are not responsible for their treatment, and you do not have to keep dangerous secrets. If you think they are in danger, tell an adult or call emergency services.

About, standards and sources

How we work

Story of U and MindOverMarks

MindOverMarks is a youth cognitive-well-being initiative. Story of U is its dedicated psychology and neuroscience publication: an independent, evidence-led publication exploring human thought, perception, identity, cognition, emotion, decision-making, social influence and behaviour. Story of U is not a peer-reviewed journal or an academic institution.

Purpose and audience

Story of U is written for curious young people aged 13 to 18, and for their teachers and schools. It is educational, not a clinical service, and does not diagnose, treat or provide therapy.

How claims are sourced

Each claim links to a source on the References page. We prefer original studies, then systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Where we only read an abstract, we say so.

Labels

Well supported, Mixed evidence, Open question, Interpretation and Explanation. They apply to one claim at a time and are our editorial judgement.

Uncertainty and disagreement

Where experts disagree we say so and describe both views. Where we are unsure, we remove the claim or label it.

What we do not do

No diagnosis, no personality tests, no scores, no tracking, and no invented endorsements, testimonials or user numbers.
